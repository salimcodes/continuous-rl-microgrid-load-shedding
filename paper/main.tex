\begin{abstract}
Adaptive load shedding on sub-Saharan campus microgrids remains dominated by fixed-priority and one-step greedy controllers. This paper benchmarks fourteen reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms against five non-learning controllers on the University of Lagos campus microgrid, a 55-building Nigerian case study, isolating the effect of action representation across three Gym environments (216-action discrete, action-masked discrete, continuous Box). All RL variants train under an equalised 60{,}000-step budget, with the five strongest trained across five seeds. Across 60 paired episodes, continuous-action Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) scores $141.44 \pm 4.96$, narrowly behind the strongest heuristic, Lowest-Priority-First ($143.55 \pm 5.91$), and ahead of an operator-grade Greedy controller ($138.90 \pm 5.64$, Holm-corrected $p<0.001$, $d=1.46$). Averaged across seeds, TD3 ($145.0 \pm 6.0$), not SAC, is the strongest continuous-control method -- a reversal from the single-seed comparison. We also audit and resolve a reviewer-identified anomaly in which a Random baseline outperformed the original Greedy controller: a discretisation-rounding bug caused Greedy to waste capacity disproportionately under severe deficit, which also reverses the original per-regime finding that RL's advantage is largest exactly there. With the bug fixed, Greedy leads SAC in four of five deficit regimes. Continuous action representation remains the decisive factor for adaptive load shedding, though its margin over strong heuristics is smaller than originally reported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Electricity supply in sub-Saharan Africa remains chronically deficient; Nigeria's national grid dispatches 3.5--5~GW against a suppressed demand of 25--30~GW \cite{iea2022,akinbulire2014}. Large institutional consumers operate as de facto microgrids that must apportion a finite, intermittent supply through controlled load shedding. The University of Lagos, with 55 buildings across eight feeders, is our case study.

Conventional controllers rely on under-frequency relays and fixed priority tables \cite{anderson1992}: auditable, but indifferent to time-varying supply. Reinforcement Learning (RL) offers an adaptive alternative: an agent observes a state $s_t$, chooses an action $a_t$, and receives a reward $r_t$ and next state $s_{t+1}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:loop}), learning a curtailment policy directly from experience \cite{sutton2018}, now a mature paradigm in the smart-grid literature \cite{vazquezcanteli2019}.

Prior load-shedding RL comparisons typically (i) omit the operator-grade one-step Greedy baseline, (ii) discretise an inherently continuous curtailment fraction, and (iii) evaluate only mild deficits, never the severe shortfalls where adaptive control matters most.

This paper closes those gaps and, in this camera-ready revision, additionally responds to a peer-review audit request. We train fourteen RL variants spanning four families on a five-month UNILAG demand record, evaluate them against five non-learning controllers under five deficit regimes with paired, Holm-corrected statistical tests, and report five headline controllers across multiple training seeds. Contributions: (i) an open Gym environment with three action-space flavours sharing identical dynamics; (ii) a reproducible, equalised-budget, multi-seed benchmark; (iii) a quantitative audit of a reward/environment interaction that can make served energy insensitive to the exact curtailment requested, together with a corrected Greedy baseline; and (iv) evidence that action-space representation, not algorithmic family, remains decisive, albeit by a smaller margin than originally reported.

\section{Related Work}
Three traditions motivate this work. Conventional load shedding relies on under-frequency relays that disconnect predefined feeders once frequency drops \cite{anderson1992} -- fast, but oblivious to which loads are shed. Optimisation-based unit-commitment formulations \cite{carrion2006} require short-horizon forecasts that are unreliable in weak-grid conditions and scale poorly with feeder count.

RL for grid and demand-response problems is surveyed by V\'azquez-Canteli and Nagy \cite{vazquezcanteli2019}. Mocanu et al.\ show deep Q-learning matches expert heuristics for residential demand response under mild deficits \cite{mocanu2019}; Ye et al.\ find continuous control beats discrete baselines in a residential multi-energy system lacking a multi-cluster priority structure \cite{ye2020}; Cao et al.\ review the broader power-system literature \cite{cao2020}. None compares an off-policy actor-critic against an operator-grade Greedy controller, nor separately reports performance under deep deficits -- gaps this paper closes.

Soft Actor-Critic \cite{haarnoja2018} and TD3 \cite{fujimoto2018} are the dominant off-policy continuous-control methods; Du et al.\ confirm continuous control benefits residential HVAC over discrete Q-learning \cite{du2021}. Tang and Agrawal argue action representation often dominates algorithm choice \cite{tang2020}; Tavakoli et al.\ propose branching architectures for high-dimensional discrete actions \cite{tavakoli2018}; Huang and Onta\~n\'on analyse invalid-action masking \cite{huang2022}, used here for MaskablePPO. We adopt these insights and show that a three-dimensional continuous Box closes nearly all of the gap between RL and the strongest heuristic. Since campus shedding must remain equitable across residences and faculties, we adopt Jain's index \cite{jain1984} as an explicit fairness reward term.

\section{System Model, MDP, and Algorithm Zoo}

\subsection{Case Study: University of Lagos Campus}
We instantiate load shedding on the UNILAG campus with $N=55$ buildings indexed $b$, each with hourly demand $d_b(t)$ (kW). An expert mapping from the campus Works and Physical Planning Department partitions buildings into $K=3$ clusters: Cluster~0 (Critical: medical, server rooms, labs, library, senate), Cluster~1 (Academic--Administrative), Cluster~2 (Residential--Commercial). Cluster demand $D_k(t)=\sum_{b\in k}d_b(t)$. Each building has priority $p_b\in\{1,\dots,10\}$, a criticality flag, and curtailment cap $c_b^{\max}\in[0,0.5]$; cluster caps $c_k^{\max}$ take the member maximum.

\subsection{RL Formulation and MDP}
We formalise the problem as a finite-horizon MDP $M=(S,A,P,R,T)$, $T=168$~h (one week). At each hour the agent observes $s_t\in S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{10}$, chooses $a_t$, and receives reward $R_t$ and next state $s_{t+1}$, learning a policy $\pi(a\mid s)$ maximising $G=\mathbb{E}[\sum_t\gamma^tR_t]$. Off-policy actor-critic methods (SAC, TD3) maintain an actor $\pi_\theta$ and critic $Q_\phi$ trained from a replay buffer; on-policy methods (PPO, A2C, TRPO) update $\theta$ along gradients estimated from rollouts; value-based methods (DQN, QR-DQN) learn $Q(s,a)$ and act greedily with respect to it.

The state is
\begin{equation}
s_t=(D_0,D_1,D_2,D_{\mathrm{tot}},P_a,\rho,\sin\omega h,\cos\omega h,a_{t-1},\rho_{t-1}),
\label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
with demands normalised to dataset maxima, $\rho=\max(0,1-P_a/D_{\mathrm{tot}})$ the deficit ratio, and $\omega=2\pi/24$ giving a cyclic hour encoding.

Three action spaces share identical dynamics. \emph{Discrete}: $A_d=\{0,\dots,L^K{-}1\}$, $L=6$, decoding to per-cluster levels $\Sigma=\{0,.1,\dots,.5\}$. \emph{Action-masked}: $A_d$ plus a per-step mask forbidding levels that exceed $c_k^{\max}$ (used by MaskablePPO \cite{huang2022}). \emph{Continuous}: $A_c=[0,0.5]^K$, used by SAC/TD3; $|A_d|=L^K$ grows exponentially in $K$, while $A_c$ has dimension $K$ and strictly contains every discrete level.

\subsection{Transition Kernel and Reward}
Demand transitions deterministically through the historical record; supply is stochastic, $P_a(t)\sim\mathcal{U}(r_{\mathrm{lo}},r_{\mathrm{hi}})\cdot D_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)$ under five regimes -- Normal (0.95,1.05), Minor (0.80,0.95), Moderate (0.60,0.80), Major (0.40,0.60), Emergency (0.20,0.40) -- drawn uniformly per training episode.

Given curtailment $c(t)$, requested service is $S_k=(1-\min(c_k,c_k^{\max}))D_k$. If $\sum_kS_k>P_a$ (oversubscription), all clusters are rescaled by $\gamma=P_a/\sum_kS_k$; \emph{the aggregate curtailed energy $D_{\mathrm{tot}}-P_a$ is then identical for every oversubscribing controller regardless of how much curtailment it requested} -- only the per-cluster split still depends on the request. This detail drives the audit in Section~\ref{sec:audit}.

The reward is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_t=\;&\omega_E\textstyle\sum_k\frac{S_k}{D_{\mathrm{tot}}}-\omega_C\sum_k\pi_k\frac{C_k}{D_{\mathrm{tot}}}-\omega_X\sum_k\frac{C_k}{D_{\mathrm{tot}}}\\
&-\omega_R\mathbb{1}[a_t{=}a_{t-1}]+\omega_FJ(\sigma_t),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
weights $(\omega_E,\omega_C,\omega_X,\omega_R,\omega_F)=(1.0,2.0,0.5,0.2,0.5)$, $\pi_k$ the critical-load share of cluster $k$, $\sigma_k=S_k/D_k$, and Jain's index $J(\sigma)=(\sum_k\sigma_k)^2/(K\sum_k\sigma_k^2)\in[1/K,1]$ \cite{jain1984}.

\subsection{Soft Actor-Critic}
SAC augments the discounted return with policy entropy \cite{haarnoja2018},
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{SAC}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\textstyle\sum_t\gamma^t(R_t+\alpha\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)))\Big],
\label{eq:sac}
\end{equation}
$\gamma=0.99$, temperature $\alpha$ tuned to a target entropy $-\dim(A_c)$. The actor is a diagonal-Gaussian network, tanh-squashed into $[0,0.5]^3$; twin critics, a 100k-transition replay buffer, batch size 128, and soft updates $\tau=0.005$ follow standard practice \cite{haarnoja2018}. All RL variants train for an equalised 60{,}000 steps (Section~\ref{sec:audit}).

\subsection{Controller Zoo and Protocol}
Table~\ref{tab:zoo} lists all controllers. Five non-adaptive baselines: Random, RoundRobin, StaticPriority (fixed 0/20/40\% per cluster), LowestPriorityFirst (proportional shed to low-priority clusters), and Greedy -- the operator-grade reference (Section~\ref{sec:audit} details its exact-lookahead implementation). Fourteen RL variants span four families: PPO \cite{schulman2017} (vanilla, wide-MLP, curriculum, tuned, and BC-warm-started from Greedy), A2C \cite{mnih2016}, TRPO \cite{schulman2015}, DQN \cite{mnih2015}, QR-DQN \cite{dabney2018}, RecurrentPPO (LSTM policy), MaskablePPO \cite{huang2022}, SAC \cite{haarnoja2018}, TD3 \cite{fujimoto2018}, and a Hybrid-PPO+Greedy ensemble routing on $\rho\ge0.4$. All implementations use stable-baselines3 \cite{raffin2021}.

The dataset spans five months (3{,}648~h, 1~Jan--31~May~2024) of hourly demand from the 55 UNILAG buildings, mean total demand 240.6~kW. Every RL variant trains for an equalised 60{,}000 steps; the five strongest (SAC, TD3, PPO-Tuned, MaskablePPO, PPO-Large) additionally train across five seeds $\{42,\dots,46\}$, reported as across-seed mean~$\pm$~std. All controllers are evaluated on the same 60 paired test episodes (168-h windows; Section~\ref{sec:audit} reports window diversity). Statistical inference uses the paired per-episode difference $d_i=R_i^{\mathrm{best}}-R_{i,j}$, giving a $t$-statistic, 95\% CI, and Cohen's $d=\mathrm{mean}(d)/\mathrm{sd}(d)$; $p$-values across the resulting comparison family are Holm-corrected \cite{holm1979}. Training and evaluation ran on a single CPU.

\section{Root-Cause Audit: The Random-vs-Greedy Anomaly}
\label{sec:audit}
Peer review of the original submission noted that Random outperformed the original, formula-based Greedy (GreedyFormula) and 11 of 14 RL variants, flagging a possible confound in the oversubscription rescale (Section III-C). We audit this directly.

\textbf{Mechanism:} GreedyFormula chose each cluster's curtailment by rounding the exact deficit-closing fraction to the nearest of six discrete levels. This rounding has no consistent direction: under-requesting curtailment is corrected for free by the oversubscription rescale, while over-requesting directly and irreversibly wastes deliverable energy. A controller sampling curtailment broadly, like Random, is more likely to land in the ``free'' regime than one tuned to sit at the boundary -- exactly where the penalty bites.

\textbf{Diagnosis:} Under the corrected, equalised-budget protocol, GreedyFormula (125.48) still trails Random (132.65), ruling out the training-budget confound (GreedyFormula has no training budget at all). GreedyFormula wastes more capacity per non-oversubscribed step (53.5~kW average) than any other heuristic -- direct evidence of the rounding bias.

\textbf{Fix:} We replace GreedyFormula with \emph{Greedy}: an exact one-step lookahead that enumerates all 216 discrete actions, applies the environment's own reward function to each (vectorised, no learned model), and selects the maximiser -- the correct operationalisation of ``greedy''. Greedy (138.90) now beats Random (132.65), resolving the anomaly, and is used as ``Greedy'' throughout the rest of this paper (Table~\ref{tab:ranking} lists GreedyFormula too, for this before/after comparison).

\textbf{Distinct evaluation windows:} The 60 test episodes draw 168-h windows from 3{,}479 possible start hours; 59 land on distinct starts (one collision), but 56 of 59 sorted-adjacent pairs overlap temporally. We disclose this as a residual limitation rather than resolve it, since doing so would require more historical data than is available.

\section{Discussion}
The audit shows the Random-beats-Greedy anomaly was a fixable implementation detail -- discretisation rounding -- not a flaw in the reward design; the oversubscription rescale is a physically necessary constraint, now reported quantitatively rather than left implicit. Fixing it also reversed the per-regime finding that RL's advantage peaks under Emergency deficit.

The corrected results support a more modest, better-evidenced claim than the original submission: SAC remains competitive with the strongest heuristics and clearly ahead of every discrete-action RL variant, but no longer straightforwardly beats a correctly implemented Greedy by a wide margin, nor is it indistinguishable from the strongest heuristic. Three practitioner takeaways follow: cast curtailment as continuous rather than discretising, since every discrete RL variant underperforms both continuous-control methods and the top two heuristics; benchmark against a \emph{correctly implemented} operator-grade baseline before crediting RL with a win, since a plausible-looking heuristic can be silently miscalibrated in a way that specifically inflates RL's apparent advantage under high-stakes conditions; and report seed-to-seed variance for any headline claim, since the SAC-vs-TD3 ranking itself reverses across seeds.

\emph{Reward-weight sensitivity.} Re-evaluating the trained SAC policy under $\omega_C\in\{0.5,1,2,4\}$ and $\omega_F\in\{0,0.5,1,2\}$ shows reward scaling monotonically with each weight (increasing in $\omega_F$, decreasing in $\omega_C$), consistent with a policy that already achieves near-maximal fairness ($J\approx0.999$) and low critical-cluster curtailment rather than trading one off against the other.

\emph{Eight feeders vs.\ three clusters.} The 55 buildings are wired across eight feeders (Section~III-A), but shedding decisions use the coarser three-cluster granularity the Works and Physical Planning Department applies operationally; the feeder count describes physical topology only, not a second control granularity.

\section{Conclusion}
We benchmarked fourteen RL variants against five non-adaptive controllers for campus load shedding on a five-month UNILAG dataset, under an equalised training budget, multi-seed reporting, and Holm-corrected statistics. SAC remains close to the strongest heuristic (141.44 vs.\ LowestPriorityFirst's 143.55) and ahead of every discrete RL variant, but its margin over a correctly implemented Greedy is $+2.54$, not the $+16.77$ originally reported, and TD3 (145.0) rather than SAC is strongest when averaged across seeds. Action-space representation, not algorithmic family, remains decisive -- every discrete variant trails both continuous methods and the top two heuristics, as originally claimed. What the corrected analysis withdraws is the stronger claim that RL decisively beats a well-implemented operator-grade baseline, particularly under severe deficit.

Future work will add a noisy multi-step power forecast to the state, scale to multi-feeder coordination via multi-agent SAC, and extend the environment with solar generation and battery storage. RL remains a credible alternative to Greedy on a sub-Saharan campus microgrid given continuous action representation, but the stronger case is competitiveness with strong heuristics and the ability to adapt online, not a large fixed performance gap.
